## Primary Abstract (recommended)

Closed-source legged robots present a deployment challenge: navigation systems issue velocity commands through manufacturer-provided SDK interfaces, but the mapping from commanded velocity to actual robot motion is opaque to the user. When commanded motion does not faithfully translate into executed motion — through deadzone behavior, under-tracking, or systematic mismatch — downstream planning decisions may rely on inaccurate assumptions about robot capability. This paper presents an offline, artifact-governed pipeline for characterizing the black-box command-to-motion response relationship of a closed-source quadruped. The pipeline converts real measurement artifacts into a schema-validated velocity response dataset, produces conservative response predictions with categorical uncertainty and reliability labels, and translates prediction evidence into advisory navigation-risk assessments with explicit warning metadata. All pipeline stages carry documented safety flags confirming that compensation, inverse command mapping, and safe command adaptation are not implemented. Evaluated on sparse forward-velocity evidence from a single K1 robot, the current implementation produces a consistent, reproducible chain of artifacts: five dataset records (four numeric, one qualitative-only), five response predictions with uncertainty labels, and five advisory risk assessments across three risk levels. The work provides an evidence-governed foundation for studying deployment-layer reliability in closed-source legged robots, establishes clear boundaries between structural artifact validation and unsupported performance claims, and identifies the repeated multi-trial experiments required before navigation outcome or safety claims can be evaluated.

## Extended Abstract (alternative)

Closed-source legged robots operated through manufacturer-provided SDK interfaces present a practical challenge for deployment reliability: navigation and planning systems issue velocity commands, but the internal controller's translation of those commands into actual robot motion cannot be inspected, modeled, or modified by the user. This command-to-motion response mismatch — manifesting as deadzone behavior at low speeds, under-tracking at higher speeds, and systematic velocity deviations — means that navigation decisions may be based on inaccurate assumptions about the robot's true motion capability. This paper presents an offline, artifact-governed pipeline for characterizing the black-box command-to-motion response relationship of a closed-source quadruped platform. The pipeline proceeds through five documented stages: construction of structured measurement artifacts from real field tests, schema-validated dataset construction that preserves qualitative evidence without numeric fabrication, a conservative rule-based response model that produces velocity predictions with categorical uncertainty and reliability labels, advisory navigation-risk mapping that translates response evidence into warning-level metadata, and claim-governed evaluation that separates supported structural claims from unsupported performance and safety assertions. Evaluated on sparse forward-velocity evidence from a single K1 robot on a single indoor surface, the current implementation produces a consistent, reproducible chain of research artifacts: five dataset records (four numeric, one qualitative-only at 0.10 m/s), five response predictions with uncertainty labels, and five advisory risk assessments across three risk levels (1 critical, 2 high-risk, 2 medium-risk), correctly identifying the low-speed deadzone and under-tracking behavior. Every pipeline output carries explicit safety flags confirming that compensation, inverse command mapping, and safe command adaptation are not implemented — a design choice that prevents the pipeline from issuing unintended robot commands. The work provides an evidence-governed foundation for studying deployment-layer reliability in closed-source legged robots, establishes a template for research reporting that stays within its evidence, and identifies the repeated multi-trial, multi-surface, and navigation outcome experiments required before performance, safety, or generalization claims can be evaluated.
