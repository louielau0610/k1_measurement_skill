## 6. Conclusion

Closed-source legged robots present a practical deployment challenge: navigation and planning systems issue velocity commands, but the robot's internal controller translates those commands into motion through a process the user cannot directly observe, model, or modify. When the commanded velocity does not faithfully translate into actual motion — whether through deadzone behavior at low speeds, systematic under-tracking at higher speeds, or lateral and yaw drift — downstream planning decisions may be built on a mismatch between assumed and actual robot capability.

This work presents an offline, artifact-governed pipeline for characterizing that command-to-motion response relationship under black-box deployment constraints. The pipeline proceeds through five documented stages: (1) construction of structured measurement artifacts from real K1 field tests, (2) schema-validated dataset construction that preserves qualitative evidence without numeric fabrication, (3) conservative response modeling that produces velocity predictions with categorical uncertainty and reliability labels, (4) advisory navigation-risk mapping that translates response evidence into warning-level metadata, and (5) claim-governed evaluation that separates supported structural claims from unsupported performance and safety assertions.

The current implementation, evaluated on sparse forward-velocity evidence from a single K1 quadruped, demonstrates that the pipeline produces a consistent, reproducible chain of research artifacts: a schema-valid dataset of five command-response records, five response predictions with uncertainty labels, and five advisory risk assessments across three risk levels. The exact-source reconstruction sanity check confirms structural consistency of the model, and the risk-mapping layer correctly identifies the deadzone at 0.10 m/s and under-tracking at higher command velocities. All pipeline outputs carry explicit safety flags confirming that compensation, inverse command mapping, and safe command adaptation are not implemented — a design choice that prevents the pipeline from issuing unintended robot commands.

These results establish an artifact-backed foundation for studying deployment-layer reliability in closed-source legged robots. They demonstrate that externally observable command-response evidence, collected through read-only SDK logging and processed within a conservative claim framework, can be transformed into structured datasets, principled predictions, and auditable advisory assessments. The claim-governance infrastructure — including the claim registry, evidence table, and non-claims — provides a template for research reporting that stays within its evidence and resists overclaiming sparse real-robot data.

Future work will expand the evidence base along several axes. Repeated multi-trial data collection is needed to evaluate held-out prediction accuracy and to estimate response variability. Multi-surface testing will assess whether response characteristics generalize beyond a single indoor floor. Extending the command grid to lateral and angular velocity dimensions will broaden the applicability of the dataset and model. Navigation task protocols with controlled outcome metrics — collision rate, near-miss rate, success rate — are required to evaluate whether advisory risk warnings correspond to real navigation outcomes. Only after such evidence exists should velocity compensation or safe command adaptation be pursued, and even then, any closed-loop control must be validated under controlled conditions before deployment.

The broader significance of this work lies not in proving superiority over existing methods but in establishing a disciplined, evidence-governed approach to a deployment problem that affects any practitioner operating a closed-source legged robot platform. By making the command-response relationship an explicit object of study rather than an assumed property of the platform, the pipeline provides a repeatable methodology for evaluating whether a given robot, on a given surface, under given conditions, can be expected to track velocity commands reliably enough for the navigation tasks its users intend it to perform.
